# Dynamic Complexity and Policy Resistance

This article scaffold supports the Systems Thinking article **“Dynamic Complexity and Policy Resistance.”** It provides synthetic data, reproducible examples, and multi-language starting points for studying compensating feedback, implementation lags, adaptive actors, metric gaming, boundary effects, and policy-resistance scenarios.

## Purpose

The repository is designed for systems-thinking education, sustainability analysis, public-interest policy learning, and reproducible modeling demonstrations. It illustrates why interventions enter adaptive feedback systems rather than passive environments.

## Structure

- `python/` — policy-resistance simulations, compensating-feedback models, adaptive actor examples, metric-gaming diagnostics, boundary-effect analysis, and implementation-lag scenarios.
- `r/` — policy-resistance plots, compensating-feedback visualization, adaptive-response tables, metric-gaming summaries, and scenario comparison.
- `julia/` — nonlinear policy-resistance and dynamic-complexity examples.
- `sql/` — schemas for policy interventions, feedback loops, actor responses, metrics, boundary costs, scenarios, and model runs.
- `rust/` — command-line diagnostics scaffold.
- `go/` — feedback-pathway utility scaffold.
- `cpp/` — efficient feedback compensation and dynamic policy examples.
- `fortran/` — recurrence-oriented policy-resistance model.
- `c/` — low-level policy feedback simulation utility.
- `docs/` — modeling principles, article notes, policy-resistance framework, assumptions, and responsible-use notes.
- `data/` — synthetic datasets.
- `outputs/` — generated outputs.
- `notebooks/` — notebook placeholders.

## Article thesis

Dynamic complexity explains why policy interventions often produce delayed, indirect, adaptive, and unintended effects. Policy resistance is not simply opposition; it is frequently evidence that feedback loops, incentives, implementation capacity, boundaries, and measurement systems were misunderstood.

## Responsible use

These examples use synthetic data and simplified models. They are for learning, exploratory analysis, and reproducible methods demonstration. They should not be used to make real policy, employment, benefit, enforcement, health, or allocation decisions without domain expertise, stakeholder review, legal review where appropriate, and careful validation against real-world evidence.
